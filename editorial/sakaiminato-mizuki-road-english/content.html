A town where the streetlights are eyeballs, the trains are covered in monsters, and statues of Japanese spirits watch you walk down the street? That sounds like an odd day out. In Sakaiminato, Tottori Prefecture, it's simply the neighborhood around the train station.

I went to see Mizuki Shigeru Road and the wonderfully strange world of GeGeGe no Kitaro. I expected the bronze yokai figures and the cute decorated trains. The surprise came when I entered Kitaro Yokai Warehouse near the station: the atmosphere suddenly turned much creepier, especially when Neko Musume appeared.

This is my walking account of the station area, with the small things I loved and the practical details that will help you visit.

Travel note: Photos and indoor impressions come from my own visit. Official road, museum and Yokai Warehouse information was checked in November 2026. Start at Sakaiminato Station — the monsters are already waiting

I hadn't even started down the sightseeing street when the town started performing. There were yokai figures near the station, and even a streetlamp styled like Medama-Oyaji, the famous eyeball character.

It's the kind of decoration that makes you look up and smile. Once I'd noticed the eyeball, I wanted to see what else the town had hidden in plain sight.

The Kitaro trains are ridiculously cute

Then there were the trains. I'd seen photographs of Kitaro-themed carriages before, but standing beside one covered in colorful characters was different.

The designs run right across the bodies of the trains. Even if you don't take a ride, it's fun to look at them at the station when they're there. Train types and times can vary, so don't build your schedule around a particular design without checking JR's current service information.

The park by the station has another monster hidden in its shape

Near the station is a park with a winding walkway. On the ground, it looks like a pleasant, slightly twisty path between the trees.

But I looked at its shape on an aerial map and had another thought. Wait. Does that look like Ittan-Momen?

The resemblance isn't an accident, according to the information in the original Japanese article: the walkway was designed with the long, cloth-like yokai in mind, with extra details added in 2020. It's one of those small jokes that is easy to miss if you only look straight ahead.

Have a look from above on a map if you're curious. It's a fun example of how seriously Sakaiminato takes its monsters.

Mizuki Shigeru Road: about 800 metres of yokai

From the station, Mizuki Shigeru Road runs toward the Shigeru Mizuki Museum. The official museum website describes a street of about 800 metres with 178 bronze yokai statues.

The characters come from the imaginative work of manga artist Shigeru Mizuki. Some look mischievous, some appear a little spooky. Yet seeing them in small bronze figures along an everyday shopping street makes many of them unexpectedly lovable.

I kept looking for the next one. You don't need to recognize every yokai to enjoy finding them.

The statues of Shigeru Mizuki himself and Kitaro are a good place to stop. Their expressions are full of character. The town isn't just selling merchandise here; the artist's imaginary world has become part of the actual streetscape.

The official museum guide to Mizuki Shigeru Road also describes evening lighting on the street. I explored the area in daytime, so that's an option to investigate rather than something I'm pretending to have seen.

Kitaro Yokai Warehouse: a different kind of monster experience

Close to the station, on the second floor of Minatosakai Exchange Center, is Kitaro Yokai Warehouse (GeGeGe no Yokai Rakuen is a different attraction—make sure you find the right place).

The version I entered was called Chapter Three, a maze-like haunted-house experience where you choose your own path. Outside it was all cute statues and cheerful trains. In here, the mood was quite different.

A lucky charm with too many choices

The ticket doubled as a special lucky charm sheet. The idea was to collect five stamps, choosing four of the blessings yourself—things like good financial luck and road safety—then completing the final mark.

I had come to meet monsters, but first I was busy choosing what kind of luck I wanted. There were too many appealing options.

The mirror room, Nurikabe and a slightly frightening Neko Musume

The route brought me into a strange mirror-filled area. Next came the familiar flat-faced yokai Nurikabe. That expression is hard to dislike.

But deeper inside, the colors and lighting made things less welcoming. Then there was Neko Musume—a character I'd always thought I knew. Under those lights, she looked unexpectedly frightening.

This was the fun of it. The outdoors had made the yokai cute. The indoor attraction reminded me that they're still supposed to be a little supernatural.

To complete my charm I had to find a prayer wheel and add the final stamp. I'll leave its location as a surprise. Finding it is more enjoyable than being told where it is.

What about the Shigeru Mizuki Museum?

If you want more background on Kitaro's creator, the Shigeru Mizuki Museum is farther along the walking street. It's an obvious next stop, but I didn't enter it on this particular visit, so I'll leave a personal museum review for another day.

The museum's official visitor guide currently lists admission at ¥1,000 for adults, ¥500 for middle and high school students, and ¥300 for elementary school students. Opening hours are 9:30 a.m.–5:00 p.m. with last entry at 4:30 p.m.

How to get to Mizuki Shigeru Road

The easy thing about this sightseeing area is that the fun starts at JR Sakaiminato Station. You can do the central statue route on foot without renting a car. Train connections generally involve the Sakai Line via Yonago; check current timetables before traveling.

We came by car on our wider San'in coastal excursion, but I wouldn't hire a car solely for the station-area walk. A car becomes useful if you're combining it with other scattered coastal or seafood stops.

For a walking route, take your time with the statues and shops rather than trying to cover every single figure at top speed. The main street is around 800 metres long, but detours and photo stops can make it a much longer experience.

What else can you do near Sakaiminato?

This is also one of Japan's best-known seafood ports. Our separate Japanese report on buying red snow crabs and sweet shrimp explains what we actually found at the local fish market. It's a different stop from the station, not part of the central walking street.

For our broader trip, we combined fishing, seafood shopping, lunch and this very strange town full of yokai. If you're visiting primarily for the statues, allow yourself enough time just to enjoy the neighborhood.

Would I come back?

Yes. The part that stays with me isn't a single famous sculpture. It's the shift in feeling: a cute train, an eyeball streetlight, one more bronze figure—and then a surprisingly creepy Neko Musume inside the warehouse.

Even if you're not a lifelong Kitaro fan, the imagination in this town is hard to resist. I would happily walk around looking for the next odd little character again.
